## A. WINTER (Cold Weather Season, Jan–Feb; Dec–Feb for NW India)

### 1. `in01_winter_map.png`
Satellite-style map of India and surroundings in January, cold blue-white tones in the north, dry clear sky.
Draw: a large HIGH-pressure area over Central Asia to the north-west of India (label `HIGH`) with its southern edge over Pakistan and
Jammu & Kashmir (isobar label `1018`); a weaker high over the Indian Ocean (isobar label `1012`, running from the Arabian Sea islands
through Kerala to Gujarat); a shallow trough along the west coast from Kerala to Gujarat (dashed line, label `TROUGH`); a shallow ridge
over Myanmar (label `RIDGE`). Surface winds as thin white arrows: north-westerly over north India, north-easterly over south India,
easterly over Assam. The sub-tropical jet stream as a thick pale-orange arrow from the west across north India along about 27°N
(label `SUBTROPICAL JET STREAM`, `27°N`, `12 km`). Three small westward-to-eastward arrows entering from the west (label
`WESTERN DISTURBANCES`). Shade the north Indian plain pale grey for fog (label `FOG`) and mark the north-west plains with a blue
outline (label `COLD WAVE`).
Labels: HIGH, 1018, 1012, TROUGH, RIDGE, SUBTROPICAL JET STREAM, 27°N, 12 km, WESTERN DISTURBANCES, FOG, COLD WAVE.

**NOTES TEXT.** In winter a vast high-pressure belt stretches from the Sahara to Siberia and India lies on its southern edge. Dry, cool
north-westerly winds blow over the north and north-easterlies over the south. The sub-tropical jet stream lies over north India at about
12 km near 27°N, with westerly winds of 80–100 kt and a maximum of about 200 kt. Five to seven Western Disturbances a month bring cloud
and rain to the north; clear skies behind them bring radiation fog and cold waves.

### 2. `in02_winter_fog_scene.png`
Cinematic ground-level scene at dawn over the Indo-Gangetic plain: dense radiation fog lying in the fields and around an airport,
runway lights glowing, a passenger aircraft on approach emerging from the fog top, clear cold sky above with a visible temperature
inversion (a sharp flat top to the fog). A thermometer-style marker is NOT wanted. Labels: `RADIATION FOG`, `INVERSION`, `CLEAR SKY`.

**NOTES TEXT.** After a Western Disturbance passes, skies clear, nights are long and cold, winds are light and the air near the ground
cools to its dew point under an inversion: widespread radiation fog. Poor visibility is the main aviation hazard in winter over north India.

### 3. `in03_western_disturbance_section.png`
West-to-east vertical cross-section over north India: a Western Disturbance (occluded front) approaching from the left, with the cloud
sequence in order: `CI`, `CS`, `AS`, `NS`, `ST` with drizzle and rain at the surface, then behind it a cold-front-type zone with `CU`
and `CB`, then clear sky. Show the Himalaya on the right. Labels: WESTERN DISTURBANCE, CI, CS, AS, NS, ST, CU, CB, HIMALAYA, WINDS BACK,
PRESSURE FALLS, PRESSURE RISES.

**NOTES TEXT.** A Western Disturbance first gives warm-front-type weather: winds back from north-west to south-west or south-east, the
temperature rises, pressure falls, and Ci thickens and lowers through As and Ns to St, with rain or drizzle. Then cold-front-type weather
follows with Cu and Cb, thunderstorms and a rise in pressure. After it passes, skies clear and cold dry northerly winds set in.

## B. PRE-MONSOON (Hot Weather / Thunderstorm Season, Mar–May)

### 4. `in04_premonsoon_map.png`
Satellite-style map of India in May, warm ochre and haze tones over the north-west, building storm clouds in the east.
Draw: a weak LOW over north-west Pakistan / Sindh (label `LOW`); a weak trough over east Uttar Pradesh and Bihar (dashed, label `TROUGH`);
a trough along the peninsula (dashed north-south line at about 78°E, label `PENINSULAR TROUGH`); a low over Myanmar; a high over the east-central Bay
of Bengal (label `HIGH`). Only two isobars (`1006`, `1008`) running parallel to the coast. Surface winds: thin arrows, south-westerly over
Rajasthan, easterly from east UP to the north-east, southerly over Odisha, West Bengal, Bihar. A red-orange glow over Rajasthan, Gujarat and
central India (label `HEAT WAVE`). A brown dust shroud over the north-west plains (label `DUST STORMS`). A cluster of storm clouds over
West Bengal, Bihar, Assam (label `NORWESTERS`). The sub-tropical jet stream, faint and shifted north to about 30°N (label `JET STREAM WEAKENS AND MOVES NORTH`).
Labels: LOW, TROUGH, PENINSULAR TROUGH, HIGH, 1006, 1008, HEAT WAVE, DUST STORMS, NORWESTERS, JET STREAM WEAKENS AND MOVES NORTH.

**NOTES TEXT.** March to May is the transition from the winter pattern to the monsoon pattern. The land heats rapidly and pressure falls
everywhere; the pressure gradient is weak, with only two isobars across the country. Violent thunderstorms, dust storms and line squalls are
common over Rajasthan and north-west India, and Norwesters (Kalbaisakhi) over the east and north-east. This is the most hazardous season for flying.

### 5. `in05_dust_storm_scene.png`
Cinematic wide shot of a dust storm over the Thar / Rajasthan plain in the afternoon: a towering wall of brown dust rolling across flat
desert, a gust front rolling ahead of it, a dark storm cloud above, a small aircraft far to the side. Labels: `DUST STORM`, `GUST FRONT`,
`STRONG SURFACE WINDS`, `VISIBILITY A FEW HUNDRED METRES`.

**NOTES TEXT.** Dust-raising winds of 30–50 kt blow between about 0900 and 1700 hr and cut visibility to a few hundred metres over a large
area. Widespread dust haze over north India can extend to 7–8 km aloft.

### 6. `in06_norwester_section.png`
Cinematic aerial-and-section view of a Norwester (Kalbaisakhi): a squall line of dark cumulonimbus with a shelf cloud advancing from the
Chota Nagpur plateau south-eastward over West Bengal and towards Bangladesh in late afternoon, lightning, a gust front over paddy fields,
hail shafts. Small inset map of east India with an arrow from the Chota Nagpur plateau to West Bengal and Bangladesh.
Labels: NORWESTER (KALBAISAKHI), CB, SQUALL LINE, CHOTA NAGPUR PLATEAU, WEST BENGAL, BANGLADESH, AFTERNOON AND EVENING, HAIL, LIGHTNING.

**NOTES TEXT.** Norwesters are pre-monsoon (hot-weather) thunderstorms of eastern and north-eastern India. They originate over the
Chota Nagpur plateau and move south-east over Bengal and Bangladesh, most often in the afternoon and evening when surface heating is at its
peak. Aircrew report line-squall development.

### 7. `in07_heat_wave_map.png`
Map of India under extreme heat: deep red and orange shading over Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and the Indo-Gangetic plain, shimmering
air above bare ground, a lone sun. A thermometer is NOT wanted; use the labels only. Labels: HEAT WAVE, RAJASTHAN, GUJARAT, MADHYA PRADESH,
NORTH INDIAN PLAINS. Do NOT write any temperature number.

**NOTES TEXT.** A heat wave is declared when the maximum temperature is at least 4 °C above normal. It is most common over Rajasthan, Gujarat
and the northern and central plains before the monsoon. (The chapter text gives no other threshold; the IMD plains threshold is a separate
number that should be confirmed before it is added.)

## C. SOUTH-WEST MONSOON (June–September)

### 8. `in08_monsoon_map.png`
Cinematic satellite-style map of India in July: dense white monsoon cloud over the west coast and the Bay of Bengal head, lush green land,
a dark low over Baluchistan. Draw: a LOW over Baluchistan (isobar `994`); the MONSOON TROUGH as a bold dashed red line from Sriganganagar through
Allahabad (Prayagraj) to the head of the Bay of Bengal; high pressure in the southern hemisphere (label `HIGH`, `1008`); winds as bold arrows
— `ARABIAN SEA BRANCH` from the south-west to the west coast and Kerala, `BAY OF BENGAL BRANCH` curving up into Bengal and then west along
the Gangetic plain; easterly to south-easterly winds north of the trough; a steep pressure gradient along the west coast. Above the map a
faint arrow band for the Tropical Easterly Jet along about 13°N (label `TROPICAL EASTERLY JET`, `15–16 km`) and a faint anticyclone over
Tibet (label `TIBETAN HIGH`).
Labels: LOW, 994, HIGH, 1008, MONSOON TROUGH, ARABIAN SEA BRANCH, BAY OF BENGAL BRANCH, TROPICAL EASTERLY JET, 15–16 km, TIBETAN HIGH.

**NOTES TEXT.** Intense heating of the land makes a low over Baluchistan and an elongated monsoon trough along the Gangetic plain, and moist
south-westerly winds are drawn in from the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. The trough lies from Sriganganagar through Allahabad to the head of the Bay,
more or less on the ITCZ, and its position governs the rainfall. At 15–16 km the Tropical Easterly Jet blows near 13°N at 70–100 kt (maximum 150 kt).

### 9. `in09_monsoon_onset_map.png`
Map of India with curved onset lines (isochrones) advancing from south to north, each line a different colour, drawn over green terrain with
cloud edges. Mark exactly these dates: `20 MAY` south Andaman Sea, `1 JUNE` Kerala, `15 JUNE` Mumbai, `1 JULY` New Delhi, `8 JULY` whole country.
Draw the withdrawal as thin dashed lines coming back: `1 SEPTEMBER` from Rajasthan, `15 SEPTEMBER` from the north-west, `15 OCTOBER` almost everywhere.
Labels: the dates above, ONSET, WITHDRAWAL.

**NOTES TEXT.** Normal onset: South Andaman Sea 20 May, Kerala 1 June, Mumbai 15 June, New Delhi 1 July; the whole country is covered by 8 July.
Withdrawal normally starts from Rajasthan by 1 September and from the north-west by 15 September, and is complete by about 15 October.

### 10. `in10_break_monsoon_map.png`
Two small maps side by side. LEFT `ACTIVE MONSOON`: the monsoon trough in its normal place along the Gangetic plain, rain over the whole country. RIGHT
`BREAK MONSOON`: the trough shifted north to the foot of the Himalaya, heavy rain only on the Himalayan foothills (Sub-Himalayan West Bengal, Nepal,
Bhutan), dry hot haze elsewhere, flood symbols over north-east India and Bihar. Labels: ACTIVE MONSOON, BREAK MONSOON, MONSOON TROUGH,
HIMALAYAN FOOTHILLS, FLOODS, DRY.

**NOTES TEXT.** In a break the monsoon trough shifts to the foothills of the Himalaya. Rainfall falls sharply over the rest of the country,
pressure rises, and weather turns pre-monsoon in type with dust haze; heavy rain on the foothills floods north-east India and Bihar.

### 11. `in11_monsoon_depression_scene.png`
Cinematic satellite-style close-up of the northern Bay of Bengal with a swirling monsoon depression, bands of thick cloud feeding rain onto the
east and central India coast, and a track arrow moving north-west. Include a second smaller disturbance over north-west India at mid-level
(a faint spiral between the Arabian Sea and Gujarat). Labels: MONSOON DEPRESSION, TRACK, MID-TROPOSPHERIC CYCLONE, HEAVY RAIN, WEST COAST.

**NOTES TEXT.** Monsoon depressions form in the north of the Bay about two or three times a month, move north-west and give well-distributed rain over
central and northern India. A mid-tropospheric cyclone between 500 and 700 hPa near 60–80°E and 18–28°N gives very heavy rain to its west and south-west,
as in Mumbai in 2005.

### 12. `in12_monsoon_strength_enso.png`
Two-panel schematic over the Pacific and Indian Ocean. LEFT `EL NIÑO`: warm water pooled in the eastern Pacific, weak south-east trade winds,
weak monsoon over India, drought symbol. RIGHT `LA NIÑA`: cold water in the east, strong trades, active monsoon over India. Labels: EL NIÑO,
LA NIÑA, WEAK TRADES, STRONG TRADES, WARM, COLD, WEAK MONSOON, ACTIVE MONSOON.

**NOTES TEXT.** In El Niño years the south-east trades weaken and the south-west monsoon is weak, and droughts in India are common (though not every
drought is an El Niño year). In La Niña years the trades are strong and the monsoon is active.

## D. POST-MONSOON / NORTH-EAST MONSOON (October–November)

### 13. `in13_post_monsoon_map.png`
Satellite-style map of India in late October: monsoon retreating (a thin dashed line near the north), a clear blue north, banded rain cloud over the
south-east. Draw: one isobar (`1010`) enclosing the whole country; the monsoon trough shifted into the Bay of Bengal with its axis along `13°N`;
a weak low off the Chennai coast; north-easterly winds sweeping from the north-east across the Bay to Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka as bold arrows (label
`NORTH-EAST MONSOON`); the sub-tropical jet stream faintly returning over Jammu & Kashmir (label `JET STREAM RETURNS`). Mark heavy rain on the coast of
Tamil Nadu and south Andhra Pradesh (label `RAIN ON TAMIL NADU COAST`).
Labels: 1010, 13°N, NORTH-EAST MONSOON, JET STREAM RETURNS, RAIN ON TAMIL NADU COAST, LOW.

**NOTES TEXT.** After the monsoon withdraws the winds change to a north-easterly regime. Pressure is flat, temperatures fall, and the north-east monsoon
brings the main rain to coastal Tamil Nadu, mostly at night. Western Disturbances (one or two a month) reach Jammu & Kashmir and Punjab, and the jet stream returns.
It is the best period for flying except over south India. (The chapter quotes two different percentages for the Tamil Nadu share; use neither until checked.)

### 14. `in14_cyclone_tracks.png`
Cinematic map of the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea showing tropical cyclone tracks as glowing curved lines with small cyclone symbols:
October storms forming near the Andaman Sea and moving towards Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha, some turning north to West Bengal and Bangladesh;
November storms in the Arabian Sea; a few that cross the peninsula, revive and strike Maharashtra or Gujarat. Colour October tracks in orange and
November tracks in blue. Labels: BAY OF BENGAL, ARABIAN SEA, OCTOBER, NOVEMBER, ANDAMAN SEA, TAMIL NADU, ANDHRA PRADESH, ODISHA, WEST BENGAL, BANGLADESH,
GUJARAT, MAHARASHTRA.

**NOTES TEXT.** Cyclonic storms are most frequent in October over the Bay of Bengal and in November over the Arabian Sea, and severe storms are
more numerous in November than October. They usually form near the Andaman Sea and move towards Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha; some strike West Bengal or
Bangladesh, and a few revive after crossing the peninsula and hit Maharashtra or Gujarat.

### 15. `in15_tropical_cyclone_anatomy.png`
Cinematic cutaway of a mature tropical revolving storm over the Bay of Bengal: the calm clear eye with sinking air, the towering eye wall, spiral rain
bands, surface inflow arrows pointing in, outflow arrows at the top pointing OUT only, storm surge rising where the eye wall meets the coast.
Labels: EYE, EYE WALL, RAIN BANDS, INFLOW, OUTFLOW, SINKING AIR, STORM SURGE. (Put the label EYE WALL on the cloud wall, not in the eye; no arrow at the top
may point towards the eye.)

**NOTES TEXT.** The eye is calm with little cloud and almost no rain; the worst weather is in the eye wall around it, with strong winds, heavy rain and
severe turbulence. Low cloud, very strong winds, heavy rain and thunderstorms are experienced when a storm crosses south India.

## E. JET STREAMS AND THE WHOLE YEAR

### 16. `in16_jet_streams_section.png`
Two stacked north-south vertical cross-sections over India from the equator to the Himalaya, height up to 18 km. TOP `OCTOBER TO MAY`: a westerly
sub-tropical jet stream core at about 12 km over north India near 27°N shown as a glowing orange tube with the arrow tail marked as a circle with a cross
(wind into the page = westerly), tropopause line above it. BOTTOM `JUNE TO SEPTEMBER`: an easterly Tropical Jet core at 15–16 km near 13°N over the
peninsula, arrow head marked as a circle with a dot (wind out of the page = easterly). The Himalaya drawn as relief at the right. Labels: OCTOBER TO MAY,
JUNE TO SEPTEMBER, SUBTROPICAL JET STREAM, TROPICAL EASTERLY JET, 12 km, 15–16 km, 27°N, 13°N, HIMALAYA, EQUATOR.

**NOTES TEXT.** From October to May the westerly sub-tropical jet (80–150 kt) lies over north India near 12 km. From June to September it is replaced by
the easterly Tropical Jet at 15–16 km near 13°N, 60–100 kt. Clear air turbulence is likely in the vicinity of either jet.

### 17. `in17_rainfall_map.png`
Map of mean annual rainfall as smooth colour gradient (deep blue for the very wet north-east, Western Ghats and Konkan coast, through green and yellow to
pale sand for the Thar). Two pins only: `MAWSYNRAM` in Meghalaya (wettest) and a pale zone `WEST RAJASTHAN` (driest). Labels: MAWSYNRAM, WEST RAJASTHAN, WESTERN GHATS,
THAR DESERT, THE HIMALAYA. Do NOT write rainfall numbers.

**NOTES TEXT.** About 75 per cent of the annual rainfall falls in the four months of June to September. West Rajasthan receives less than 13 cm in a year, while
Mawsynram in Meghalaya receives about 1187 cm.

### 18. `in18_four_seasons_overview.png`
A 2×2 grid of four small maps of India with identical outlines. TOP-LEFT `WINTER (JAN–FEB)`: north-westerly winds, high pressure to the north-west, jet stream over north India.
TOP-RIGHT `PRE-MONSOON (MAR–MAY)`: heat low in the north-west, dust and Norwesters. BOTTOM-LEFT `SW MONSOON (JUN–SEP)`: monsoon trough, two branches. BOTTOM-RIGHT
`POST-MONSOON (OCT–NOV)`: north-east winds, cyclones, rain in Tamil Nadu. Use the matching colour mood of each season (cool blue, hot orange, lush green, soft amber).
Labels: the four season titles above; no other text.

**NOTES TEXT.** The India Meteorological Department recognises four climatological seasons: winter (January–February), pre-monsoon or hot weather (March–May),
the south-west monsoon (June–September) and the post-monsoon or north-east monsoon season (October–November).
