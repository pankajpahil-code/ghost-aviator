How to Become a Pilot in India

Draft byline: Capt. Pankaj Pahil

Every few weeks a young person messages me some version of the same question: "Sir, I want to become a pilot — where do I even start?" Usually they have already read ten different answers on ten different websites, and every one of them contradicts the last. So let me give you the honest version, in the order it actually happens, from someone who has flown the line and taught on the ground.

Becoming a pilot in India is not one exam or one course. It is a ladder of licences, and a set of written papers, and a medical, and a lot of hours in an aeroplane — and they do not happen one after another. They overlap. The students who finish fastest are the ones who understand that on day one.

The ladder: SPL, PPL, CPL

Three licences, each one earning you the right to do more:

LicenceWhat it isWhat it lets you do
SPL
Student Pilot Licence		 Your permission to begin training at a flying club Fly with an instructor, and eventually solo under supervision. It is not a licence to carry anyone.
PPL
Private Pilot Licence		 The first real licence Fly as pilot-in-command privately, carry friends and family — but not for money.
CPL
Commercial Pilot Licence		 The professional licence Be paid to fly. This is the one that puts you in the right seat of an airliner after a type rating.

Almost everyone reading this wants the CPL. Understand that the CPL is the end of the ladder, not the beginning — and that the written exams you keep hearing about belong to the CPL stage, which is why students spend so long on them.

Step 1 — Check you are actually eligible

Before you spend a rupee, confirm two things:

  • 10+2 with Physics and Mathematics from a recognised board. If you took commerce or arts, this is not the end of the road — you can clear Physics and Maths through an open school such as NIOS and you are back in the game. Thousands have done exactly that.
  • Your board verification. Not the marksheet — the official verification letter from your board. I have watched more applications die on this single document than on any exam. Start it early; boards are slow.

Step 2 — Get the medical done early. Earlier than you think.

This is the single piece of advice I most wish every student took seriously. The medical is the one requirement that can end the dream outright, and it is the cheapest step of the whole journey. So do it first, before the fees, before the flying, before the coaching classes.

There are two stages:

  • Class 2 medical — the entry gate. Done at a DGCA-approved examiner. Clear this before you commit money to training.
  • Class 1 medical — the professional standard, required for the CPL. Done at an approved centre, and considerably more thorough: eyes, ears, ECG, blood work, the lot.
From the cockpit: if you wear spectacles, that alone does not disqualify you — there are limits, and plenty of professional pilots fly with correction. But find out where you stand from an approved examiner, not from a forum. And if something is borderline, you want to know in year zero, not after twenty lakh rupees.

Step 3 — Your DGCA computer number

You cannot sit a single DGCA paper without a computer number. It is your identity in the examination system, generated through the eGCA portal after your board verification is accepted.

I have written the whole process out separately, including the mistakes that get applications rejected: How to apply for a DGCA computer number.

And there is no negative marking on DGCA papers. A wrong answer costs you exactly what a blank costs you: nothing extra. So never leave a box empty — a blank is a guaranteed zero, a guess is free. Fill in every question before you walk out.

The trap nobody warns you about: the Navigation paper is not just "navigation". It pools Air Navigation, Radio Navigation and Instruments into one 100-question paper. Students prepare general navigation beautifully, walk in, and meet a block of radio-aids and instrumentation questions they never studied. Prepare all three as one subject, because that is how you will be examined.

Seventy percent is the pass mark on every paper. That is a high bar and it is not negotiable — which is exactly why practising full-length papers under the clock matters more than re-reading notes. Everything you need for that is free here: the question bank, full-length exam mode, and previous-year papers.

Step 5 — RTR(A): the licence that surprises people

The RTR(A) is what legally permits you to operate an aircraft radio and speak to ATC — and without it, you are not working a radio, whatever else is in your logbook. It comes in two halves: a written examination, and a practical in which an examiner plays air traffic control and you talk to him on the frequency. Pass mark on the practical is 50%.

One thing to note, because most pages online are still wrong about it: the RTR(A) examination was conducted for decades by the WPC wing of the Ministry of Communications. Since November 2025 the DGCA conducts it, under the Radio Telephone Operator (Restricted) Certificate and Licence Rules, 2025 and CAR Section 7 Series 'G' Part VI. Good news for you: sittings are far more frequent than the old three-batches-a-year cycle. (The WPC still licenses the aircraft's radio apparatus — that part did not change.)

The practical is where good students fall down, because you cannot learn radiotelephony by reading it — you have to say it out loud until the phraseology is automatic. That is precisely why this site has a radio simulator: real ICAO phraseology, an examiner who probes you when you miss a read-back, emergencies, and a gradesheet at the end. The theory behind every call is in the complete RTR(A) book, also free.

Step 6 — The flying

Ground exams get you a pass. Hours get you a licence. You will fly at a DGCA-approved flying training organisation, building from dual instruction, to solo, to cross-country, to instrument and night flying — and then a skill test with an examiner.

The choice everyone agonises over — India or abroad. Training overseas can be faster and sometimes cheaper per hour, with better weather and less waiting. But foreign hours come home needing conversion, and conversion has its own exams and paperwork. Neither route is "the smart one"; they trade money against time against complexity. Decide it with a spreadsheet, not with a brochure — the cost calculator is a fair place to start.

Step 7 — After the CPL

The CPL is a licence to be paid to fly — it is not, by itself, an airline job. What follows is a type rating on the aircraft you will actually operate, then selection with an operator. Many pilots also add an instrument rating and, later, a multi-engine endorsement or an instructor rating. The ATPL sits further out again, earned with experience, and its written papers are the same discipline as the CPL papers you are about to sit — which is why the ATPL material here is built on the same foundation.

What I tell every cadet

Three things, and then go and start.

One: do the medical first. It is the cheapest step and the only one that can stop you outright.

Two: run the exams and the flying in parallel. The students who clear papers while they build hours finish a year ahead of the ones who queue everything up neatly. Weather cancels flying; a cancelled slot is a study session.

Three: money is a plan, not a surprise. Sit down and cost the whole thing — hours, ground school, medicals, exam fees, and the eighteen-odd months you have to eat and live through. Do it before you begin, and add a buffer, because something always costs more than the brochure said.

This site exists so that the ground-study half of that plan costs you nothing at all. Notes, question bank, mock exams, past papers, the radio simulator — free, for every student pilot in India, permanently. Use all of it.

Fly safe. And start with the medical.
