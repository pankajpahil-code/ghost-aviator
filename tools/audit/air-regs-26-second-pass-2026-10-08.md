## The three flagged rows

**Row 10 (id 359), wake turbulence just after take-off. Key: C, high angle of attack and high gross weight.**
No DGCA, ICAO or AAI text was found. The only source opened is American: FAA AIM 7-4-3, read on faa.gov
today. It says weight, speed, wingspan and wing shape govern vortex strength, that strength rises with
weight and with lower speed, and that it is greatest when the aircraft is "HEAVY, CLEAN, and SLOW".
That rules out A (engine thrust) and B (gear and flaps increase it; the FAA says a dirty configuration
hastens decay). "Angle of attack" does not appear in that FAA section: C is right on weight, and the
angle-of-attack half is inference (slow and heavy means high angle of attack). Searched and not found:
the 354 DGCA CARs (the word vortex appears only in syllabus lists), AIP India, Doc 4444.

**Row 15 (id 464), day VFR fuel for "any propeller driven aeroplane". Key: B, destination + 30 minutes.**
B matches the general-aviation CAR exactly: CAR Section 8 Series O Part III (Issue IV, 13 Apr 2022)
2.2.3.6.1 c), day VFR, "final reserve fuel for at least 30 minutes at normal cruising altitude" (line 1119).
But the stem does not say general aviation. For commercial air transport, CAR Section 8 Series O Part II
4.3.6 has no day-VFR rule and sets final reserve at 45 minutes for a reciprocating-engine aeroplane
(lines 1702, 1718), which is option A's shape. B is the best-supported answer because only the GA CAR
has a day-VFR fuel rule. The Captain may prefer to add "general aviation" to the stem.

**Row 26 (id 905), flicker from rotor or propeller blades. Key: B, stroboscopic vertigo, make small RPM changes.**
The old key A ("cannot cause any harm") is wrong: ICAO Doc 9683 lists flicker vertigo among recognised
hazards (lines 14916, 14980 of the extracted text). But the question has a third option, C ("nausea,
dizziness or unconsciousness, hence land as soon as possible"), and no document opened today decides
between B and C. Doc 9683 gives no symptoms or remedy. FAA pages that would (Pilot's Handbook, AC 90-89)
returned 403 and were not read; a search summary attributes the symptoms in C to the FAA handbook and the
RPM advice in B to AC 90-89. That is a summary, not a source I opened.

### Notes

1. **Row 9.** The site's explanation cites the Investigation Rules 2025 r.4(1). The text opened today is the
   **draft** (G.S.R. 310(E), May 2025), where r.4(1) reads "not later than twenty-four hours". The final
   notification, G.S.R. 829(E) of November 2025, was **not** opened; secondary summaries say it keeps 24
   hours. The key stands on the DGCA CAR either way.
2. **Row 12.** This is proved by absence. The whole CAR was searched for "to the left": the only hits are
   circuit turns (3.2.x, "make all turns to the left"), interception signals, and light angles. The old
   key C matches an old UK rule, not an Indian or ICAO one.
3. **Row 23, and a side finding.** For a 28-year-old both sources give twelve months. For pilots over 40
   they differ: r.39C(5) as held on disk says validity is halved after forty, while CAR 7-C-I 5.1(a) says
   one year up to 60 and six months above 40 only for single-crew operations. The Aircraft Rules text on
   disk is an older compilation. Any question about a pilot over 40 should be checked against the current
   rule before it is trusted.

  Row 16's option reads "All crew Passengers" and row 17's stem starts with a stray "46".

  Later revisions were not looked for.

  says the same.
- ICAO Doc 9683 was read from a copy hosted by the Swiss civil aviation office, not from ICAO's store.
