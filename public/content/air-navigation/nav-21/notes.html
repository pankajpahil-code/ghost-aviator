Definition of Departure

What is Departure?

In spherical navigation, Departure is the East–West linear distance between two meridians, measured along a specified parallel of latitude, expressed in Nautical Miles (NM). Because it is always measured along a parallel of latitude, departure always represents a Rhumb Line distance.

Departure: The E–W distance between two meridians along a given parallel of latitude.
Unit: Nautical Miles (NM)  |  Track type: Rhumb Line

Why Does Departure Change with Latitude?

The Earth is a sphere. All meridians converge and meet at the North and South Poles. Two meridians always subtend the same angle (ch.long) at the pole, but the actual physical distance between them decreases as latitude increases. At the Equator, meridians are furthest apart; at the Poles, they meet at a single point.

🔑 The Extremes:
Equator: 1° ch.long = 60 NM (= 60' arc of a Great Circle) — MAXIMUM
Poles: 1° ch.long = 0 NM (meridians meet at the Pole) — ZERO
Meridians converge toward the poles: longitude is constant, distance is variable.

The Departure Formula

The Fundamental Formula

Because departure varies as the cosine of latitude, the fundamental formula for East–West distance in NM is:

Departure (NM) = ch.long (minutes) × cos (latitude)
ch.long — change in longitude. Convert DEGREES → MINUTES (degrees × 60) first!

cos(lat) — scaling factor. Accounts for meridian convergence at that latitude.
⚠ CRITICAL RULE — never skip this step!
ALWAYS convert ch.long from degrees to minutes before calculating.
Example: 20° change of longitude = 20 × 60 = 1200 minutes of arc.

Scenario 1: Forward Calculation — Finding New Position

Worked Example 1 An aircraft at 60°00'N 005°22'W flies 165 km due East. Find the new position.

1. Standardise units: 165 ÷ 1.852 = 89 NM (this is our Departure)
2. Apply the formula: 89 = ch.long(min) × cos 60° = ch.long × 0.5 → ch.long = 89 ÷ 0.5 = 178 min
3. Convert to degrees: 178 ÷ 60 = 2°58' | Direction = East (+)
4. Plot new position: 005°22'W + 2°58'E → ✓ New Position = 60°00'N 002°24'W

Scenario 2: Reverse Algebra — Finding Latitude

Worked Example 2 In which latitude is a difference of longitude of 44°11' equivalent to a departure of 2000 NM?

1. Convert ch.long to minutes: 44°11' = (44 × 60) + 11 = 2651 min
2. Isolate cos(lat): 2000 = 2651 × cos(lat) → cos(lat) = 2000 ÷ 2651 = 0.7544
3. Find the latitude: lat = arccos(0.7544) = ✓ 41° NORTH or SOUTH
💡 Why North OR South? cos(41°N) = cos(41°S) — the cosine function gives identical values for equal latitudes in both hemispheres. Hence TWO valid answers!

Scenario 3: The Dual-Latitude Problem — Departure at a Different Latitude

When a multi-leg route crosses into a different latitude, the same change of longitude translates to a different physical E–W distance. Two methods solve it:

Worked Example 3 Aircraft leaves G (40°S). Flies 240 NM South → 240 NM West → 240 NM North to K. Find the Rhumb Line distance from K to G.

Method 1 — Double substitution (3 steps):
1. 240 NM S = 4° lat change → lat of H = 44°S
2. 240 = ch.long × cos 44° → ch.long = 240 ÷ cos 44° = 333.6 min
3. Back at 40°S: Dep = 333.6 × cos 40° = 255.6 NM

Method 2 — Ratio Formula (1 step, PREFERRED):
Dep(40°S) = 240 × (cos 40° ÷ cos 44°) = 240 × (0.766 ÷ 0.719) = ✓ 255.6 NM — same answer!
Same meridians, different parallel — the E–W distance grows toward the Equator.
🔑 THE RATIO FORMULA — master this!
Dep(A) ÷ cos(A) = Dep(B) ÷ cos(B)   →   Dep(A) = Dep(B) × (cos A ÷ cos B)
Use when you know departure at one latitude and need it at another.
4
The Box Pattern Trap

Understanding the Trap

A classic DGCA exam question involves an aircraft flying a box pattern: equal distances in four cardinal directions (S, E, N, W). The trap is assuming the aircraft returns to its starting point. It does NOT!

The Westward leg is flown at a higher (poleward) latitude than the Eastward leg. At higher latitudes meridians are closer together, so the same distance crosses MORE degrees of longitude Westward than Eastward. The aircraft ends at the same latitude but further West (in the Northern Hemisphere).

Worked Example 4 — The Classic Box Pattern Aircraft at 27°N 170°W flies: 3000 km S, 3000 km E, 3000 km N, 3000 km W. Final position?

The no-math logic sequence:
1. Westward leg is at 27°N (HIGHER latitude) — meridians are CLOSER together.
2. Eastward leg is at 0° Equator (LOWER latitude) — meridians are FURTHER apart.
3. Same 3000 km westward crosses MORE meridians (more ch.long) than eastward.
4. Net result: same latitude (27°N), displaced WESTWARD.
✓ ANSWER: 27°00'N 173°18'W — NOT 170°00'W!
Equal legs do not close the box — the aircraft ends WEST of its start (Northern Hemisphere).
💡 EXAM SHORTCUT — inspect the options:
• Same latitude as start? YES (same N/S distance flown) — eliminate options at a different latitude.
• Displaced WEST in the Northern Hemisphere (W leg at higher lat).
• Displaced EAST in the Southern Hemisphere (W leg at lower lat).

Kinematics Integration (Speed + Time + Geometry)

Combining Flight Performance with Departure

DGCA questions often combine flight mechanics (TAS from Mach number, Ground Speed from wind) with departure geometry. The approach is always two-phased: first find the departure distance using kinematics, then use the departure formula to find the new position.

Worked Example 5 — Kinematics + Departure Aircraft leaves 36°00'S 130°14'E. Track 270° (due West). M 0.81, Temp 0°C, 35 kt tailwind. Fly for 18 minutes. Find the new position.

Phase 1 — Kinematics (finding the departure distance):
1. TAS from Mach: LSS at 0°C ≈ 644 kt → TAS = 0.81 × 644 ≈ 520 kt
2. Ground speed: Track 270° with 35 kt tailwind → GS = 520 + 35 = 555 kt
3. Distance: 18 min at 555 kt = (18 ÷ 60) × 555 = 166.5 NM = Departure (Westward)

Phase 2 — Geometry (finding the new position):
1. 166.5 = ch.long(min) × cos 36° = ch.long × 0.809
2. ch.long = 166.5 ÷ 0.809 = 206 min = 3°26' West
3. 130°14'E − 3°26' = ✓ New Position: 36°00'S 126°48'E
🔑 MACH → TAS QUICK REFERENCE (TAS = Mach No. × Local Speed of Sound):
LSS at +15°C (ISA sea level) = 661 kt | LSS at 0°C ≈ 644 kt | LSS at −20°C ≈ 620 kt | LSS at −56.5°C (tropopause) ≈ 573 kt
⚠ Common error: 661 kt is the speed of sound at +15°C (ISA sea level), NOT at 0°C. LSS = 38.94 × √(temperature in Kelvin). Using 661 kt at 0°C overestimates TAS by ~15 kt.
6
Navigator's Master Summary

Core Rules — Never Forget These!

RULE 1: Departure is MAXIMUM at the Equator (1° ch.long = 60 NM) and ZERO at the Poles.
RULE 2: Departure varies as the COSINE of latitude. As latitude ↑, departure ↓.
RULE 3: ALWAYS convert ch.long to MINUTES (× 60) before using the formula.
RULE 4: Departure always represents a RHUMB LINE distance (along a parallel).
RULE 5: In the box pattern: same N/S distance → same latitude, but displaced West (NH) / East (SH).
RULE 6: Use the Ratio Formula for dual-latitude problems: Dep A = Dep B × (cos A ÷ cos B).

DGCA Practice Questions with Full Solutions
Q1 A flight is made along the parallel of latitude from A (48°00'N 004°00'W) to B (48°00'N 002°27'E). Calculate the distance.

ch.long = 4°00'W to 2°27'E = 6°27' = 387 minutes
Departure = 387 × cos 48° = 387 × 0.6691 = ✓ 259 NM
Q2 An aircraft flies 1000 NM along 090°(T) from C (36°00'N 174°45'E) to D. What is the longitude of D?

1000 = ch.long(min) × cos 36° → ch.long = 1000 ÷ 0.809 = 1236 min = 20°36' East
174°45'E + 20°36' = 195°21'E → crosses the anti-meridian → ✓ Longitude of D = 164°39'W
Q3 Starting from E (50°N), an aircraft flies EF 000°(T) 300 NM; FG 090°(T) 300 NM; GH 180°(T) 300 NM. Find the Rhumb Line bearing and distance from E to H.

E→F: 300 NM North → lat of F = 55°N
F→G: 300 NM East at 55°N → ch.long = 300 ÷ cos 55° = 523 min = 8°43'
G→H: 300 NM South → lat of H = 50°N (same as E, displaced East)
Departure at 50°N = 523 × cos 50° = 336 NM → ✓ Bearing 090°(T), Distance 336 NM
Q4 Track and distance along 80°S from 176°15'W to 179°45'E?

ch.long (westward across the anti-meridian) = 3°45' + 0°15' = 4° = 240 minutes
Departure = 240 × cos 80° = 240 × 0.1736 = 41.7 NM → ✓ Track 270°(T), Distance 41.7 NM
Q5 In which latitude is a difference in longitude of 44°10' equivalent to a departure of 2295 NM?

44°10' = 2650 minutes | cos(lat) = 2295 ÷ 2650 = 0.866 → ✓ Latitude = 30° North or South
Q6 Aircraft J (36°S 130°14'E) at 0946Z, Track 270°, FL100, Temp 0°C, M 0.81, 35 kt tailwind. Position at 1004Z?

Time flown: 1004 − 0946 = 18 min | TAS: M 0.81 at 0°C ≈ 520 kt | GS = 520 + 35 = 555 kt
Departure = (18 ÷ 60) × 555 = 166.5 NM West | ch.long = 166.5 ÷ cos 36° = 206 min = 3°26'
130°14'E − 3°26' = ✓ 36°00'S 126°48'E
Q7 (a) At which latitude does departure (NM) equal (i) the difference in longitude in minutes, (ii) HALF the difference in longitude in minutes? (b) At 52°N, what are the Rhumb Line and shortest distances between 136°16'W and 043°44'E?

(a)(i) cos(lat) = 1 → lat = 0° → ✓ the EQUATOR   (ii) cos(lat) = 0.5 → ✓ 60° N or S
(b) ch.long = 136°16' + 43°44' = 180° = 10800 min → Rhumb Line = 10800 × cos 52° = ✓ 6650 NM
Shortest = Great Circle over the Pole = (90° − 52°) × 2 = 76° × 60 = ✓ 4560 NM
Q8 From L (37°S): LM 270°(T) at 310 kt for 80 min; MN 180°(T) at 280 kt; NP 090°(T) at 250 kt for 93.5 min. P is due South of L. Find distance and time for leg MN.

L→M: 310 × 80/60 = 413.3 NM West at 37°S → ch.long = 413.3 ÷ cos 37° = 517 min = 8°37'W
N→P: 250 × 93.5/60 = 389.6 NM East, covering the same 517 min → cos(lat N) = 389.6 ÷ 517 = 0.7536 → lat N = 41°S
M→N: 41°S − 37°S = 4° South = 240 NM | Time = 240 ÷ 280 × 60 = ✓ 240 NM, ≈ 51 minutes
Q9 Aircraft Q (GS 301 kt) flies along 46°N through 10° of longitude. Aircraft R (GS 364 kt) flies between the same meridians in the same time. At what latitude is R?

Q: 600 min × cos 46° = 417 NM | Time = 417 ÷ 301 × 60 = 83 min
R: 364 × 83/60 = 503.5 NM in the same time → cos(lat) = 503.5 ÷ 600 = 0.839 → ✓ Latitude of R = 33° N or S
Q10 (MCQ) Aircraft at 50°N 006°E flies 300 NM South, 300 NM East, 300 NM North, 300 NM West. Final position relative to start?

East leg at 45°N (wider meridians), West leg at 50°N (narrower) → same distance West crosses MORE longitude → ✓ (c) WEST of the start point
Q11 (MCQ) Aircraft at 04°10'S 178°22'W flies North 2950 NM, turns left 90°, then flies 314 km Rhumb Line. Final position?

2950 NM North = 49°10' of latitude → 45°00'N | Left turn = West. 314 km = 169.5 NM at 45°N
ch.long = 169.5 ÷ cos 45° = 239.7 min ≈ 4°00'W → 178°22'W + 4°00' = 182°22'W → ✓ (d) 45°00'N 177°38'E
Q12 (MCQ) An aircraft at 60°N flies eastward around the Earth at 240 kt. What ground speed must a second aircraft fly along the Equator to circle the Earth in the same time?

Circumference at 60°N = equatorial × cos 60° = HALF the equatorial distance
Speed ratio = 1 ÷ cos 60° = 2 → 240 × 2 = ✓ (c) 480 knots
