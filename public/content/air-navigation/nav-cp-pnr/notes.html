Why We Need Two Points

Picture a long sea crossing. Halfway across, something goes wrong: an engine runs rough, a passenger collapses, smoke appears in the cabin. You have two questions, and you need the answers before the emergency, not during it:

  • "Which way is quicker — go on, or turn back?" The Critical Point (CP) answers this. Before the CP, home is quicker. After it, the destination is quicker.
  • "How far can I go and still be able to come home?" The Point of No Return (PNR) answers this. Up to the PNR you still have the fuel to return to base. Beyond it you are committed to the destination, whatever its weather does.

Keep the two apart in your head from day one. The CP is about time. The PNR is about fuel. One tells you the quicker way out of trouble; the other tells you the last place you can still change your mind.

When are they used?

A CP is worked out for any long leg with few diversions, such as over water or remote terrain, and for twin-engine operations a long way from an adequate aerodrome. A PNR is worked out when the destination is isolated, for example an island with no alternate within reach, so the only fall-back is the airfield you left.

The Critical Point (CP)

The CP is the point on track from which it takes the same time to fly on to the destination as to fly back to the departure point (or to either of two chosen airfields).

Building the formula: three letters

SymbolMeaning
DTotal distance between the two airfields (NM)
OGroundspeed on (continuing towards the destination)
HGroundspeed home (returning towards departure)
XDistance from departure to the CP

At the CP the time on equals the time home:

(D − X) ÷ O = X ÷ H

Cross-multiply: H(D − X) = OX, so HD = X(O + H), which gives:

X = D × H ÷ (O + H)
Distance from departure to the CP. Time to the CP = X ÷ groundspeed out.

Memory hook

The distance to the CP is the whole distance multiplied by the Home groundspeed, divided by the sum of the two. Home goes on top. A fast way home lets you push further out before turning back is no longer the quicker option.

Worked Example 1: CP with a headwind out

A to B is 1200 NM. TAS 400 kt. Wind component on the way out is 50 kt head. Find the distance and time from A to the CP.

O = 400 − 50 = 350 kt. H = 400 + 50 = 450 kt (the headwind becomes a tailwind going home).
X = 1200 × 450 ÷ (350 + 450) = 540 000 ÷ 800 = 675 NM.
Time to CP = 675 ÷ 350 = 1.929 h = 1 h 56 min.
Check: on to B = 525 NM at 350 kt = 1.5 h. Back to A = 675 NM at 450 kt = 1.5 h. Equal, so the answer is right.
CP is 675 NM from A, reached after about 1 h 56 min.

Wind and the CP

  • Still air: O = H, so X = D ÷ 2. The CP is exactly halfway.
  • Headwind out: the CP moves past halfway, towards the destination. In the example above it is 675 NM of a 1200 NM leg.
  • Tailwind out: the CP comes back before halfway, towards departure.

The one-line rule

The CP moves INTO the wind. The stronger the wind, the further it moves. If an answer puts a headwind CP short of halfway, it is wrong before you calculate anything.

Why? Into a headwind, going on is slow and coming home is fast. To make both times equal, you have to be further along, closer to the slow side.

Engine-Failure CP

A CP drawn for an emergency is normally worked out for the worst case, which is one engine out. After a failure the aircraft drifts down and flies at a lower TAS, both on and home. Two rules decide which speed goes where:

  • Where is the CP? Use the engine-failure TAS to find O and H, because that is the speed you will fly after the failure, whichever way you go.
  • When do I reach it? Use the normal (all-engine) groundspeed out, because until the failure happens you are flying normally.

Worked Example 2: Engine-failure CP

A to B is 2000 NM. Normal TAS 450 kt, engine-failure TAS 330 kt. Wind component out is 40 kt head throughout. Find the distance and time to the engine-failure CP.

Engine-failure groundspeeds: O = 330 − 40 = 290 kt, H = 330 + 40 = 370 kt.
X = 2000 × 370 ÷ (290 + 370) = 740 000 ÷ 660 = 1121 NM.
Normal groundspeed out = 450 − 40 = 410 kt. Time = 1121 ÷ 410 = 2.735 h = 2 h 44 min.
Check: 879 NM on at 290 kt = 3.03 h; 1121 NM home at 370 kt = 3.03 h.
Engine-failure CP: 1121 NM from A, reached after 2 h 44 min.

Classic mistake

Using the engine-failure groundspeed for the time to the CP. You only fly that slowly after the failure. The clock to the CP runs at normal cruise speed.

The Point of No Return (PNR)

The PNR is the furthest point along track from which the aircraft can still return to its departure airfield using its safe endurance. Safe endurance is the time the aircraft can fly without touching the reserve fuel it must still have on arrival.

Building the formula

Let E = safe endurance, T = time out to the PNR. The time home is then (E − T). The distance out equals the distance home:

T × O = (E − T) × H

So TO + TH = EH, which gives:

T = E × H ÷ (O + H)
Time out to the PNR. Distance to the PNR = T × O.

Notice what is missing

The distance to the destination does not appear in the PNR formula at all. The PNR depends only on endurance and the two groundspeeds. If a question gives you the A-to-B distance for a PNR, it is there to tempt you. You need D for the CP, never for the PNR.

Worked Example 3: PNR with a tailwind out

Safe endurance 6 h, TAS 240 kt, wind component out 30 kt tail. Find the time and distance to the PNR.

O = 240 + 30 = 270 kt. H = 240 − 30 = 210 kt.
T = 6 × 210 ÷ (270 + 210) = 1260 ÷ 480 = 2.625 h = 2 h 37.5 min.
Distance = 2.625 × 270 = 708.75 ≈ 709 NM.
Check: home 708.75 NM at 210 kt = 3.375 h. Out + home = 2.625 + 3.375 = 6.0 h, the whole safe endurance.
PNR: 709 NM from base, after 2 h 37.5 min.

Wind and the PNR

Work Example 3 again in still air: T = 6 × 240 ÷ 480 = 3 h, distance = 720 NM. Now with a 30 kt headwind out: O = 210, H = 270, T = 6 × 270 ÷ 480 = 3.375 h, distance = 3.375 × 210 = 708.75 NM, exactly the same distance as with the tailwind.

  • Still air gives the greatest PNR distance.
  • Any wind reduces it, head or tail, because you lose more on the slow leg than you gain on the fast one.
  • A headwind and a tailwind of the same strength give the same PNR distance, but not the same time. Into wind it takes longer to get there.
  • The stronger the wind, the bigger the reduction.

Contrast with the CP

Wind moves the CP (into the wind). Wind shrinks the PNR distance, whichever way it blows. An exam option saying "a tailwind out increases the distance to the PNR" is wrong.

PNR from Fuel Instead of Time

Larger aircraft plan in kilograms, not hours, and burn fuel at different rates going out (heavier, perhaps a different level) and coming back. Turn each fuel flow into fuel per nautical mile:

Fuel per NM = fuel flow (kg/h) ÷ groundspeed (kt)
Then: distance to PNR = usable fuel ÷ (fuel per NM out + fuel per NM home)

The logic is the same as before. The fuel used going out to the PNR plus the fuel used coming home must add up to the fuel you are allowed to use (total fuel minus reserves).

Worked Example 4: PNR from fuel

Usable fuel (reserves already removed) 24 000 kg. TAS 420 kt, wind component out 30 kt head. Fuel flow out 5200 kg/h, fuel flow home 4800 kg/h.

O = 390 kt, H = 450 kt.
Fuel per NM out = 5200 ÷ 390 = 13.33 kg/NM. Fuel per NM home = 4800 ÷ 450 = 10.67 kg/NM.
Distance = 24 000 ÷ (13.33 + 10.67) = 24 000 ÷ 24.00 = 1000 NM.
Time = 1000 ÷ 390 = 2.564 h = 2 h 34 min.
Check: 1000 × 13.33 = 13 333 kg out, plus 1000 × 10.67 = 10 667 kg home, total 24 000 kg.
PNR: 1000 NM, 2 h 34 min from base.

Exam Traps

  1. Wind sign. "Wind component −50" or "50 kt head" on the outbound leg means O = TAS − 50 and H = TAS + 50. Reverse the sign for the way home and the whole answer flips.
  2. Distance or time? Read the last words of the stem. Many options are built from the right working stopped at the wrong step: the time when the distance was asked, or the distance from the wrong end. "Distance to B from the CP" is D − X, not X.
  3. Hours and minutes. 8 h 30 min is 8.5 h, not 8.3 h. Convert first, or work in minutes throughout.
  4. Safe endurance vs total endurance. Only the safe endurance goes into the PNR formula. If you are given total endurance and a reserve, subtract the reserve first.
  5. The PNR ignores the destination distance. See section 6.
  6. Engine-failure CP. Engine-failure TAS for the position, normal groundspeed for the time.
  7. Sanity check. CP with a headwind out is past halfway. PNR with any wind is shorter than the still-air figure. Use this to strike out options before you calculate.

Practice Questions

Work each one before you open the answer. Every answer is checked by plugging it back into the definition.

Q1A to B is 900 NM, TAS 300 kt, still air. Where is the CP?
Answer: 450 NM from A
O = H = 300 kt, so X = 900 × 300 ÷ 600 = 450 NM, exactly halfway.
Q2A to B is 800 NM, TAS 250 kt, wind component out 50 kt tail. Find the distance from A to the CP, and the distance from the CP to B.
Answer: 320 NM from A; 480 NM to B
O = 300 kt, H = 200 kt.
X = 800 × 200 ÷ 500 = 320 NM from A (short of halfway: tailwind out, so the CP moves back into wind).
CP to B = 800 − 320 = 480 NM.
Check: 480 ÷ 300 = 1.6 h on; 320 ÷ 200 = 1.6 h home.
Q3Using Q2, how long after leaving A is the CP reached?
Answer: 1 h 04 min
320 NM at groundspeed out 300 kt = 1.067 h = 1 h 04 min.
Q4Safe endurance 5 h, TAS 200 kt, still air. Find the distance to the PNR.
Answer: 500 NM
T = 5 × 200 ÷ 400 = 2.5 h. Distance = 2.5 × 200 = 500 NM. In still air you go out for half the safe endurance.
Q5Same aircraft as Q4 with a 40 kt headwind out. Find the time and distance to the PNR.
Answer: 3 h 00 min, 480 NM
O = 160 kt, H = 240 kt.
T = 5 × 240 ÷ 400 = 3.0 h.
Distance = 3.0 × 160 = 480 NM, less than the still-air 500 NM.
Check: 480 ÷ 240 = 2.0 h home; 3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0 h.
Q6Same aircraft again with a 40 kt tailwind out. What is the PNR distance, and why does it match Q5?
Answer: 480 NM
O = 240 kt, H = 160 kt. T = 5 × 160 ÷ 400 = 2.0 h. Distance = 2.0 × 240 = 480 NM.
The distance equals Q5's because swapping O and H leaves O × H ÷ (O + H) unchanged. Only the time differs (2 h here, 3 h into wind).
Q7Total endurance 7 h 30 min, of which 1 h 30 min is reserve. TAS 300 kt, wind component out 60 kt head. Find the time to the PNR.
Answer: 3 h 36 min
Safe endurance = 7.5 − 1.5 = 6 h.
O = 240 kt, H = 360 kt.
T = 6 × 360 ÷ 600 = 3.6 h = 3 h 36 min (distance 3.6 × 240 = 864 NM).
Using the total 7.5 h by mistake gives 4.5 h, a typical wrong option.
Q8Usable fuel 18 000 kg. Groundspeed out 400 kt at 6000 kg/h; groundspeed home 480 kt at 4800 kg/h. Find the distance to the PNR.
Answer: 720 NM
Fuel per NM out = 6000 ÷ 400 = 15 kg/NM. Fuel per NM home = 4800 ÷ 480 = 10 kg/NM.
Distance = 18 000 ÷ (15 + 10) = 720 NM.
Check: 720 × 15 = 10 800 kg out, plus 720 × 10 = 7 200 kg home, total 18 000 kg.
