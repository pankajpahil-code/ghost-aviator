Plus the RTR(A), which is a separate certificate with its own examination structure — covered further down, because it catches people out.

Why "every paper is 90 minutes" is wrong. The examination authority's own instructions to candidates permit a candidate to leave for a break after one hour, and forbid it in the final thirty minutes. A rule like that only makes sense for a paper longer than ninety minutes. The structure of the rulebook itself refutes the blogs.

The single biggest misunderstanding: the Navigation paper

If you take away one thing from this page, take this.

The Navigation paper is not one subject. It is three. It pools

  • Air Navigation — the classical navigation syllabus: direction, charts, dead reckoning, the 1-in-60 rule, time, convergency, flight planning;
  • Radio Navigation — radio waves and propagation, ADF/NDB, VOR, ILS, DME, radar, SSR, area navigation and satellite systems;
  • Instruments — pressure instruments, gyroscopics, compasses, air data computers, the flight-instrument and avionics syllabus.

One hundred questions, three hours, drawn from all three. Every year students prepare general navigation to a very high standard, sit down, and find a block of radio-aids and instrumentation questions they simply never opened a book on. They do not fail because they were weak; they fail because they prepared one third of a paper.

Treat those three as one subject from the first day. On this site they are three separate study sections for teaching reasons — Air Navigation, Radio Navigation, Instruments — but exam mode pools them exactly as DGCA does, which is the version you should be practising against once your chapters are done.

Seventy percent, and what that really means

Every paper is 70% to pass, and there is no aggregate — each subject must be cleared on its own. Sit with that number for a moment.

On a 50-question paper you may get fifteen wrong. On a 100-question paper, thirty. That is not a generous margin on a syllabus this wide, and it changes how you should prepare: knowing a topic "roughly" is worth almost nothing. Either you can answer a question on it under time pressure or you cannot.

Which is why, past a certain point, more reading stops helping and only timed practice does. The sequence I give my own students:

  1. Read the chapter and take the chapter quiz immediately — same sitting, while it is fresh.
  2. When a subject's chapters are done, sit a full-length paper under the clock in exam mode. No pausing, no looking things up.
  3. Work the previous-year papers for that subject. Question style repeats even when the questions do not.
  4. Go back only to the topics that actually cost you marks. Your dashboard tracks your weak chapters across attempts so you are not guessing at what to revise.

There is no negative marking. Answer every single question.

DGCA papers carry no negative marking. A wrong answer costs you nothing that a blank answer does not cost you equally.

So let me be blunt about what that means in the hall: a blank answer is a guaranteed zero; a guess is a free lottery ticket. Leaving a question unanswered is simply throwing away marks. Even on a four-option question you know nothing about, a guess is worth 25% of a mark on average — and if you can eliminate two options, it is worth half a mark.

The technique I teach: work the paper in three passes. First pass, answer everything you know cold. Second pass, work the calculations you need paper for. Third pass, with whatever minutes remain, fill in every remaining blank — eliminating what you can, then choosing. Never walk out of a DGCA paper with an empty box on your sheet. Not one.

RTR(A) — now a DGCA examination

Worth stating clearly, because a lot of pages online are still out of date on this. The RTR(A) — the certificate that legally lets you operate an aircraft radio and speak to ATC — was for decades examined by the WPC wing of the Ministry of Communications. Since November 2025 the DGCA conducts the RTR(A) examination, under the Radio Telephone Operator (Restricted) Certificate and Licence Rules, 2025, together with CAR Section 7 Series 'G' Part VI.

Two practical consequences for you: sittings are much more frequent than the old April / August / November cycle, and the written paper runs in Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Kolkata and Chennai. Structure is unchanged: a written paper and a practical in which an examiner plays air traffic control and you work the frequency. The practical pass mark is 50%.

One distinction worth knowing, because it is examinable: the WPC is still India's radio spectrum and apparatus regulator — it licenses your aircraft's radio station and runs NFAP and SACFA. What moved to the DGCA is the operator's examination.

Reading will not get you through the practical. Speaking will. There is a full breakdown in the RTR(A) exam guide, the complete theory in the RTR(A) book, and a radio simulator that grades your actual transmissions against ICAO phraseology — the only tool of its kind in India, and free.

Subject by subject — what each paper really tests

Air Navigation (with Radio Nav and Instruments)

The most feared paper, and fairly so — it is the widest. It rewards a working pen and paper: the 1-in-60 rule, triangle of velocities, chart scale and properties, time and convergency, plus every radio aid and every flight instrument. It punishes anyone hoping to reason it out on the day. Start here.

Technical General

Aerodynamics, engines, airframes, systems, and principles of flight — 100 questions in 3 hours. Conceptual rather than numerical, which fools students into skimming it. The questions are precise, so your understanding has to be. Start here.

Aviation Meteorology

Fifty questions, and the most learnable paper on the list — the syllabus is finite and the question patterns are stable. Atmosphere, pressure, wind, cloud, stability, icing, thunderstorms, visibility, and reading actual METAR/TAF. If you are looking for a paper to clear early and build confidence, this is it. Start here.

Air Regulations

Fifty questions, and the paper students most often underestimate. It is dense with specific values — separation minima, licensing provisions, ICAO annex material, rules of the air — and it does not reward intuition. It rewards revision. Start here.

Technical Specific

Fifty questions on the actual aircraft type you are being examined on: its systems, limitations, performance and handling. This is where knowing your aeroplane matters rather than aeroplanes in general. Start here.

How to actually plan the year

Two things, and they are the whole strategy.

Run the papers in parallel with your flying, not after it. Weather will cancel flying slots — that is not lost time, that is a study session that was handed to you. Students who queue everything neatly finish a year behind.

Clear one paper properly before opening the next. Two half-prepared subjects is the most common way to fail two papers in one month. Pick one, finish it, sit it, then move.

And this, above everything: a wrong answer you learned from a cheap question bank is worse than a question you never saw, because you will walk into the exam confident and wrong. Every answer published on this site is verified against an authoritative reference before it goes live, and anything that cannot be verified is dropped rather than guessed.
