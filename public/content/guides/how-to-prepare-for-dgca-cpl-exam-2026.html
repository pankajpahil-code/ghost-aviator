Most "how to prepare" pages online give you a motivational list. This one gives you an order of attack. The DGCA CPL written exams are a set of separate papers, each passed on its own, and the students who get through them fastest are not the ones who study the most hours. They are the ones who choose the right order, finish one paper at a time and practise under the clock.

Each paper is assessed on its own with no aggregate, and there is no negative marking. RTR(A) also has a practical Part 2, which is a voice/ATC test rather than a written paper. The full detail is in the DGCA CPL exam pattern guide.

Step 0: sort out your computer number

You cannot register for DGCA papers without a Flight Crew computer number on the PARIKSHA portal. Do it first, because it is the one step you cannot study your way around. Follow the computer number guide. If you are still deciding whether to become a pilot at all, start with how to become a pilot in India.

The roadmap, subject by subject

Take one paper at a time, and sit it before you open the next. The order below is a suggestion built from how learnable each paper is, not a rule. If your flying school or your type-rating timeline forces a different order, follow that.

1. Aviation Meteorology: clear it first

Fifty questions on a finite syllabus with stable question patterns, so it is the best paper to build confidence on. Work through the Meteorology chapters, drill each chapter's questions in the Meteorology question bank, and practise reading real METARs and TAFs rather than only the theory.

2. Air Regulations: revise, do not guess

Fifty questions packed with specific values: separation minima, licensing provisions, ICAO annex material and rules of the air. Intuition does not help here, repetition does. Use the Air Regulations chapters and the Air Regulations question bank, and come back to the same chapters more than once.

3. Navigation: three subjects in one paper

The Navigation paper has 100 questions in 3 hours and pools Air Navigation, Radio Navigation and Instrumentation. Prepare all three from the start, with a pen and paper for the calculations. Practise each in its question bank: Air Navigation, Radio Navigation, Instrumentation.

4. Technical General: precise concepts

One hundred questions in 3 hours on aerodynamics, engines, airframes and systems. It is conceptual rather than numerical, which tempts students to skim it. Study the Technical General chapters and test yourself in the Technical General question bank.

5. Technical Specific: know your aircraft

Fifty questions on the aircraft type you are being examined on. The type-specific material on this site is built around the Diamond DA-42 NG, so check the Technical Specific chapters and the question bank against your own type, and use your school's approved manual for anything this site does not cover.

6. RTR(A): start the speaking early

RTR(A) is a separate certificate with a written Part 1 and a practical Part 2. Reading will not pass the practical, speaking will. Learn the theory from the RTR(A) book and the RTR(A) question bank, then practise your transmissions in the Radio Simulator. The RTR(A) exam guide and the DGCA takeover guide explain the process.

The routine for every subject

  1. Read or watch one chapter. Use the chapter notes and, where available, the video lecture.
  2. Take that chapter's questions straight away, in the same sitting while it is fresh.
  3. When the subject's chapters are done, sit a full timed paper in the free DGCA mock test. No pausing, no looking things up.
  4. Work the previous-year papers for the subject. The style of questions repeats even when the questions do not.
  5. Go back only to the chapters that cost you marks. The dashboard tracks weak chapters across your attempts if you sign in.

How long each step takes depends on your flying schedule, your background and how many hours a day you can give. Weather-cancelled flying slots are free study sessions. Do not borrow someone else's timetable.

On the exam day

  • Answer every question. There is no negative marking, so a blank is a guaranteed zero and a guess is not.
  • Work in three passes: answer what you know cold, then do the calculations that need paper, then fill every remaining blank with your best elimination.
  • Know the break rule. The examination authority's instructions to candidates permit a break after the first hour and forbid one in the last thirty minutes.

Mistakes that cost students a year

  • Two half-prepared papers at once. Finish and sit one before opening the next.
  • Preparing one third of Navigation. General navigation alone will not carry a paper that also tests radio aids and instruments.
  • Trusting an unverified question bank. A wrong answer learned with confidence is worse than a question you never saw. Hold every source to the standard in how answers are verified.
  • Only reading. Past a certain point, timed practice moves your score and more reading does not.
